A hunter's handbook — the Shattered Coast

Hunters of the Shattered Coast

Welcome aboard, hunter. Twelve pirate lords rule the Shattered Coast, and every port, wreck and drowned bell tolls their names. You hunt them — one lord at a time, one voyage at a time — until all twelve are done.

Welcome aboard — what is this game?

You and your friends play a company of hunters: seasoned heroes who have hunted the Twelve across the Shattered Coast for years. Each voyage targets a different lord — that week's lord is the voyage's monster. You follow clues, protect the people caught in the middle, and strike when you know enough to hurt him.

One of your group is the Chronicler, the storyteller. They play that week's Pirate Lord, his fleet, and everyone you meet in every port. Everyone else plays a hunter — that's you, and this site is yours.

What you need: two six-sided dice, your character sheet, and your company gathered around the table. That's the whole kit. If you can roll two dice and tell a good story, you can play — no experience needed, and nothing to learn first.

The hunt, in one sentence: find this week's lord, learn what can end him, and be brave enough to use it — then move on to the next of the Twelve.

Your hunter

Your hunter is the hero you play. You build them from a playbook — a kind of hunter, like the Foretold, the Cutpurse, or the Battle-Mage. There are 28 playbooks, all together in one list, and each one plays differently: different strengths, different moves, a different way of seeing the Coast.

Your sheet holds everything about your hunter:

  • Ratings — five numbers that say what you're good at: Charm (grace and guile — talking, charming, lying, singing your way out), Cool (nerve under fire — keeping steady when everything is going wrong), Sharp (wits and eyes — noticing things, figuring things out, reading people and places), Tough (strength and steel — fighting, hauling, standing your ground), Weird (magic and the uncanny — sorcery, curses, strange gifts, things beyond the natural). You pick one ratings line on your playbook; the numbers run from −1 to +3.
  • Look — what your hunter looks like, picked from the lists on your playbook or written in your own words.
  • Gear — your weapons and kit, each with tags that say what it does (a cutlass might be 2-harm hand; a heavy crossbow 3-harm far reload).
  • Moves — the things you can do. Every hunter has the eight basic moves below. Your playbook gives you its own moves on top — pick the ones that fit your hunter, and they show up on your sheet.
  • Luck, harm and experience — the three tracks that keep score of your fortune, your wounds, and what you've learned. They are explained in full below.

Your hunter also carries a scar, a famed deed, and an old debt — the marks of a life already lived. Write them on your sheet; the ports already know them.

How a roll works

When you do something risky, roll two six-sided dice and add the rating the move names. That's the whole game, really.

  • 10+ — a clean success. You do it, and it goes your way.
  • 7–9 — a partial success. You do it, but there's a cost, a compromise, or an ugly choice.
  • 6 or less — a miss. It goes wrong — and you mark 1 experience, because hunters learn fastest from failure.
  • 12+ — an advanced hit. Better than a plain success. Each move says what extra you get.

A +1 forward means +1 on your next roll. A +1 ongoing means +1 while the situation lasts. Hold is something you bank now and spend later, as the move says.

The basic moves

Short versions, for the table. The full versions — with the 12+ results, the full question lists, and the Work Sorcery glitch list — are on the Moves Reference page.

Roll two six-sided dice and add the rating named. 10+ is a clean success. 7–9 succeeds at a cost, a compromise or an ugly choice. 6 or less is a miss: mark experience, and trouble arrives. Ratings: Charm (grace and guile), Cool (nerve under fire), Sharp (wits and eyes), Tough (strength and steel), Weird (magic and the uncanny).

Persuade Someone (+Charm)

Talk a person into something with a promise, a threat, a lie or a song. On 10+ they do it if you keep your word; on 7–9 they want proof or payment first. Against fellow hunters, 10+ gives them experience to agree and +1 forward if they do.

Keep Your Nerve (+Cool)

Act under pressure: swing across on a burning rope, hold a door against boarders, lie to a lord's harbourmaster. On 10+ you do it; on 7–9 you face a worse outcome, a hard bargain or an ugly choice.

Lend Aid (+Cool)

Help another hunter. On 10+ they take +1 to their roll; on 7–9 they still do, but you share whatever goes wrong.

Search for Clues (+Sharp)

Investigate a place, a wreck, a body, a ledger or a rumour. On 10+ ask the Chronicler two questions from the list; on 7–9 ask one, but the search costs time, draws eyes or leaves a trace. What happened here? What is being hidden? What is not what it seems? Where did they go? What can hurt or stop it? What was it guarding?

Read the Room (+Sharp)

Size up a tense moment — a tavern, a parley, a deck before the board. On 10+ ask three questions; on 7–9 ask one. Who is really in command? Who wants to run? What is my best way in or out? Who is most vulnerable? What should I watch for? Acting on an answer gives +1.

Fight (+Tough)

Trade harm in a brawl, a duel or a boarding action. On 10+ deal your weapon's harm and take less in return, or deal +1 harm; on 7–9 you deal harm and take theirs in full. On a miss, you are in their hands.

Guard Another (+Tough)

Shield a person, a place or your ship. On 10+ hold 3; on 7–9 hold 1. Spend hold to suffer the harm instead, keep your charge from harm once, or draw the enemy onto you.

Work Sorcery (+Weird)

Cast, pray, curse or commune. Choose an effect: harm at a distance, heal, bar a place against a named foe, scry a person or place, dispel a curse, summon aid, or bind a spirit. On 10+ it works cleanly; on 7–9 it works, but choose a glitch: it draws attention, it is temporary, it costs you, or it works too well.

Harm, luck and experience

  • Harm. You have 7 harm boxes on your sheet. Armour reduces the harm you take. At 4 or more harm you are unstable: your wounds get worse without care. Healing comes from rest, chirurgeons, shrines and sorcery — rarely in the middle of a fight.
  • Luck. Spend a luck point to turn a miss into a 12, as if fate itself stepped in — or to shrug off all the harm from one blow. When your luck runs out, the sea collects: expect the worst, at the worst moment.
  • Experience. Mark experience on every miss, and whenever a move or your history says so. Fill 5 boxes to level up: erase them and choose an improvement from your playbook.

A voyage — how one voyage flows

Every voyage follows the same tide:

  1. Pick up the trail. Word reaches the company — a raided port, a vanished tribute ship, a chart that should not exist. This week's lord has left his mark somewhere, and the trail starts there.
  2. Follow the clues. Search wrecks, ledgers and rumours; read the room in every tavern and parley. The port folk — fisherfolk, merchants, lighthouse keepers, tavern keepers, pressed sailors — are the people caught between you and the Twelve. They know things, if you earn their trust.
  3. Find what can end him. Every lord has a weakness. Every voyage should bring you one step closer to finding it, testing it, or keeping it safe. They can't just be out-fought — fleets have tried.
  4. Strike. Armed with the weakness, the company moves. Without it, he slips away laughing.
  5. Sail on. Wounds are bound, tales are told, and the next lord's name is already on the wind. One lord down, eleven to go — until all twelve are done.

Word travels at the speed of sail and horse. A warning sent by raven can be shot down. A getaway needs a tide, not a key. Blades, bows, boats and bells only — that is the whole of the Coast's arsenal, and it has always been enough. Sea beasts and drowned things still sail here — under their flags, or in their wake. Ask whose colours they fly.

The Twelve Lords — one lord, one voyage

There are twelve pirate lords, and the campaign is twelve voyages — one lord at a time. Each voyage, the company picks up the trail of a different lord, and that lord is the voyage's monster: find what can end him, strike, and move on to the next.

Your ship — home, haven and getaway

Your company has one good ship, and she is yours. Give her a name and a home port, and pick her kind: brig, sloop, cog, longship or cutter.

Her qualities — pick three: fast, and she outruns anything but a lord's flagship; shallow-drafted, for creeks and smugglers' coves; heavily gunned for her size; warded against curses and scrying; a roomy hold for cargo, prisoners or refugees; unremarkable, so no one looks twice; or a crew of twelve loyal salts.

Hull harm: at 4+, she is breaking up — get her to a friendly yard, fast. Name your first mate, and anyone else aboard worth naming. Company funds and prize money: track it loosely, in marks — a full purse, a lean purse, or debts at the harbourmaster's. Prize money from a lord's captured ships is real income, and every prize crew you sail home is one less ship hunting you.

Already a seasoned hero?

If you are bringing a hero from another campaign — a D&D 5e level 13 character, say — you don't start fresh. Bring the legend, not the stat block. These sheets capture who they are and what they are famed for, and the seasoned start below makes sure they arrive feeling that powerful.

The seasoned start — everyone takes all of this:
  • Ratings: pick a line on your playbook, then raise any two ratings by +1 (no rating above +3). Seasoned heroes are good at what they do.
  • Extra moves: take your playbook's starting moves, plus two more moves from its list.
  • Improvements: take three improvements now. One of them may be an advanced improvement from your playbook's list — including a move borrowed from another playbook, if it fits your story.
  • Luck: start with 6 of 7 luck. You already burned one, long ago, surviving the thing that made you famous.
  • A scar, a famed deed, an old debt: invent one of each — a limp or a hand that shakes near bells; a deed the ports still sing about; a debt someone can collect. Write them on your sheet.

What carries over from your old character

  • One signature treasure. Your best magic item crosses over, translated into tags. A flaming greatsword might be 2-harm hand messy fire; boots of elvenkind become quiet, +1 to sneak. Agree the tags with the Chronicler; the item keeps its name and history.
  • Your spellcasting style. Keep how your magic looks and what it costs you. The moves define the effect — you define the flavour.
  • One ally and one enemy. Name a henchman, mentor, lover or contact who still aids you — and one surviving foe. Your old enemy now sails with one of the Twelve. One of the Twelve made them an offer.
  • Shared history, doubled. When you reach the History section on your playbook, pick two entries for each other hunter, not one. This company has bled together for years.
  • Wealth and standing. You are not rich — the hunt eats gold — but you are known. Ports open gates for your names, and their spies report your movements.

What does not cross over: hit points, armour class, spell slots and saving throws stay in the old world. If your hero could fly, teleport or raise the dead every morning, that power is now rare, costly and story-shaped — agree with the Chronicler what it looks like as a move before the first voyage.
